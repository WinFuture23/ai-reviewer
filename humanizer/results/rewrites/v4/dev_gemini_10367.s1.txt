I peered through.

I had expected darkness, or a hidden room. What I found was a pair of eyes, and I knew them from the portrait in the foyer. They belonged to Amber Maria Hawkins, a beautiful, spirited woman who had supposedly "fled to the continent" years before. The woman behind the wall had not fled. She had been entombed.

Her eyes were no longer human. They were the color of clouded amber and glowed with a fierce spite. She did not blink. She did not scream. She looked, and her gaze had weight. Where it landed on the opposite wall, the wallpaper blistered and turned to grey ash.

They tell me Steepleford is no longer on the maps. The postal carriages bypass the valley entirely, and the heavy mists have reclaimed the geography of the place. I am an old man now, Frederick, of a lineage that once valued logic and the hard certainties of the law, and I am afraid. It has been forty years. I must record the truth of Salter's Lane, of Amber Maria Hawkins and of the man, if he can be called such, named Mr. Polleto. If I do not, the sickness that started in that soil may one day find its way into the wider world, and humanity is ill-equipped for a blight that consumes not the flesh but form itself.

### The Boar's Head

"I'll go no further than the Boar's Head, sir," the carriage driver said. He kept glancing up the rising slope of Salter's Lane. "The air up there... it doesn't sit right in the lungs."

He refused to take me past the village green. That was the autumn of 1882. I was a young solicitor, sent to settle the accounts of the Hawkins estate, and I dismissed his warning with the arrogance of youth. You would have done the same. The village lay tucked into a fold of the hills, and it was silent. Not the quiet of the countryside: a heavy, pressurized stillness. I walked the remaining mile toward the Hawkins manor alone.

The trees of Salter's Lane were ancient oaks and elms, and in that season they should have been gold. They were a uniform grey. They were not dead in any ordinary sense. A light breeze stirred as I passed, and a branch as thick as a man's thigh did not break or sway. It dissolved. It crumbled into a fine powder that hung in the air like a veil of ash. I reached out to the trunk of a great oak, and my finger passed through the bark as if it were made of compressed smoke. There was no wood beneath, only a desiccated, calcified dust.

By the time I reached the manor, my black coat had turned grey.

Every morning, in London, I sweep a fine grey dust from my pillow. My doctors call it a skin condition, a remnant of some "valley fever" I contracted in my youth, and you would likely agree with them. I never went back. I resigned from the firm and moved to the heart of the city, for the density of its stone and the permanence of its iron. I am not whole.

### The Porch

"Mr. Frederick," said the man on the porch. "You have come for the ledgers. But the ledgers are written in a language of loss that you may find difficult to balance."

His voice did not seem to come from his throat; it vibrated in the air around him. This was Mr. Polleto, ostensibly the "caretaker," hired by the distant cousins of the Hawkins line. He was tall, dressed in a charcoal suit so deep it seemed to absorb what little light there was. His skin had the quality of wet parchment. His eyes were too large for his face and shimmered with a gelatinous, unblinking intensity. Looking at him gave me vertigo, and as he spoke I became violently nauseated, as if my very cells were recoiling from him. The grass at his feet was not merely dead. It had turned translucent, the blades become a clear, brittle glass that shattered under the weight of his shadow.

"The town is unwell, Mr. Polleto," I managed. I was clutching my briefcase.

"The town is transitioning," he said, with a thin, lipless smile. "Change is often mistaken for malady by those who cling to the old shapes."

He led me inside and showed me to a room on the second floor. In the center of the house, a large section of the hallway had been bricked over, crudely, violently. The mortar was fresh. The bricks themselves looked ancient, as if they were aging centuries in a matter of days.

### One Missing Brick

That night the Steepleford Cough took hold of me. I had already heard it from the cottages in the village: a dry, rattling sound, as if the villagers were breathing in ground glass. I could not sleep. The house groaned, and it was not the settling of timber. It was a moan. From behind the bricked-up wall came a rhythmic thudding, and I took my candle into the hallway.

Near the eye-level of a tall woman, a single brick was missing.

She had been bricked in alive by the town elders, perhaps by Polleto himself, for some perceived transgression. The blight on Salter's Lane was no disease of the soil. It was her vengeance, her rage turned into a literal force of erosion. She was looking through the house, through the hills, at the trees until they forgot how to be wood, at the people of Steepleford until their lungs forgot how to hold air.

She is still staring. Somewhere, in a place that no longer exists on any map, she sits behind a wall of weeping brick, her amber eyes fixed on the horizon, unmaking the world. Sometimes, when my study is very quiet, I hear the sound of a brick being tapped from the inside, and the walls feel remarkably thin. The blight is not gone.

"She is quite dedicated, is she not?"

Mr. Polleto stood at the end of the corridor. He held no candle, yet a faint, sickly luminescence clung to his skin and made him visible.

"You did this," I gasped, and fell against the wall. The stone under my hand was soft, like rotting fruit. "You let them bury her."

"I did not suggest it." He stepped closer, and the floorboards shriveled beneath him. "I merely facilitated the equilibrium. I am a catalyst, Frederick. I accelerate what is already there. The town held a secret rot; I simply gave it the agency to manifest. Amber Maria provides the will; I provide the medium."

He reached out a hand. The fingers were elongated, their tips fading into a grey mist. I was terrified. He was not a man, nor even a ghost. He was entropy, the end of things, in a charcoal suit.

"The trees were the first to go," he went on, his voice now a chorus of dry whispers. "They are simple structures. Humans are more complex. They take longer to turn to dust. But look at your hands, Frederick. Look at the edges of your life."

### The Green

I do not know how I found the strength to move. I lunged past him. He did not try to stop me; he watched with those unblinking, gelatinous eyes, as if my escape were a temporary delay in an inevitable process. The stairs crumbled like sandbags under my boots. I burst out of the front door and into Salter's Lane.

The moon was out. The trees were no longer recognizable as trees. They were pillars of ash, held together by the lack of wind, and the air my body displaced as I ran brought the oaks down behind me. A hundred-year-old grove vanished into grey soot as I passed, a silent avalanche of dust.

Mr. Polleto is walking now. He is walking toward the cities, toward the crowds, carrying the silence of Salter's Lane with him: the guest who never leaves, the shadow that dissolves the floor, the man who watches while the trees turn to dust.

In the village the lights were dim. At one window I saw a woman whose features were blurring, her nose and chin softening into a featureless mask of grey skin. On the green I found my carriage driver slumped over his seat, dead, and not a corpse in any way I understood. His clothes were draped over a pile of fine white powder. I grabbed the reins and whipped the horses. They were terrified; their hooves struck the ground with a muffled, hollow sound. We drove through the mist and the falling ash until the air began to bite with the cold, honest sting of a winter frost.

When Polleto told me to look at my hands, I had looked down. My fingernails were translucent. The skin of my wrists was beginning to flake away into that same fine, grey powder.
