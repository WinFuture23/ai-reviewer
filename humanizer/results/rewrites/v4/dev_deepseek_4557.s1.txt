“No, not the ’65. The ’66 is singing tonight, I can feel it in the stones.”

Justin Mac Carthy said it to a table of guests, and he was proven right. His wine pairings had become uncanny, his sense of when a bottle stood at its peak legendary. The guests marvelled. His hospitality, famous already, became more famous still.

The fifth letter of resignation in as many months lay on the polished oak desk in the library at Ballinacarthy, County Cork.

“*…and whilst I have the utmost respect for your person and your household, sir,*” Justin read aloud, in a rich baritone, “*the persistent and…* *aethereal*… *interference in the wine cellar makes the execution of my duties quite impossible. I regret to inform you that I shall be departing at first light. Yours, etc.*”

The letter came from Higgins, a butler who claimed to have forged his nerves of steel in the service of a Bengal Lancer. Justin was exasperated. He sighed, and the applewood in the hearth crackled over the sound.

He was a squire in his prime, known across three counties for his open door, his roaring fires and, above all, the quality of his claret. The Mac Carthy cellars, a cool, vaulted labyrinth of stone beneath the house, held vintages that could make a Frenchman weep. Now they were haunted.

No groaning spectres, no clanking chains. Casks turned up tapped, not smashed, but with a neat, drilled hole. The finest bottles left their designated bins and reappeared in puzzling, almost artistic patterns. A ’45 Lafite was found perched atop a barrel of porter like a ruby crown. And from somewhere among the racks came the faint echo of a satisfied chuckle and the rustle of tiny, quick feet.

His butlers, proud and unflappable professionals every one, went down with their minds made up and came back frightened. One swore he had been pinned by his own coattails to a rack of ’63 Burgundy. Another claimed a battalion of corks had bombarded him like tiny cannonballs. Higgins, the last of them, mumbled only about “a little man with a face like a wrinkled walnut and an impudent grin,” and packed his bags. Believe whichever of the three you like.

O’Sullivan, the quiet man who took the post after Higgins, would sometimes see from the corner of his eye the master sitting on an upturned crate deep in the vaults, seemingly talking to a barrel. Now and then he found two glasses set out where only one had been fetched. He was far too well-trained to mention it.

Justin poured himself a whisky from the decanter on the sideboard, not from the cellar. “Enough,” he said to the empty room. He would beard this lion, or this leprechaun, in its den.

He took no candle. The cellar had modern gas lamps, and they hissed as they always did. He unlocked the heavy iron-bound door and went down the worn stone steps. The lamps threw a yellow light over the ranks of barrels and the silent battalions of bottles, their shoulders dusty with time.

He stood and listened for a long moment. The drip of a distant tap. The house settling. Perhaps it was all fancy, a collective madness.

Then he saw it. On the floor before the oldest cask, a pre-Phylloxera claret he called The Patriarch, lay a tiny, perfect puddle of ruby wine. Beside it stood a thimble.

One winter’s night, a thimble of The Patriarch would be poured by the light of a single guttering candle, the gas lamp politely turned off.

“You know,” said a voice like dry leaves, quiet, looking up at the vaults, “they think we’re all gone. The Cluricaunes. That men like you, with your ledgers and your science, have no room for us anymore.”

Justin swirled the ancient wine in his glass. “Then they’ve never tasted a wine cared for by one,” he said. “Ballinacarthy has room. For as long as the vines grow and the barrels age.”

The little figure nodded, took a minuscule sip and smiled, content, with no mischief in it at all. He lifted the thimble. “To the wine.”

“To the guardian of the wine,” Justin said. Two glasses met, one large, one small. Above them, the house slept.

On other nights Justin brought down his new acquisitions and listened intently to the verdicts, delivered with the withering authority of a Parisian wine critic. They had become friends. More than once the little man pointed out a cask with a taint no one else could detect, and saved Justin a fortune.

That first night, though, Justin was annoyed, and amazed, and close to laughing. He strode forward, his boots echoing. “Alright,” he called, firm but not unkind. “The game’s up. Show yourself. You’ve terrorised my staff and pilfered my best stock. I’ll have a word.”

Silence. Then, from directly behind a barrel of amontillado: “Terrorised, is it? And pilfered? A harsh tongue for a Mac Carthy, and you a man famed for his hospitality.”

Justin spun. On the spigot of the amontillado barrel sat a figure no taller than his boot, in a coat of faded green and breeches of brown leather, with a hat that might once have been pointed and was now crumpled like a forgotten mushroom. Higgins had got the face right: a wrinkled walnut, with two sparkling eyes. Slung over his shoulder like a soldier’s rifle was a tiny, finely wrought silver spigot.

“Naggeneen,” he said, tipping the hat. “Cluricaune of Ballinacarthy Cellars, at your service. Though it seems my service, keeping your barrels sound and your wine from turning to vinegar, isn’t appreciated.”

Justin surprised himself with a slight bow. “Justin Mac Carthy. And I appreciate sound wine well enough. It’s the midnight tastings and the assault on my staff I could do without.”

“Assault!” Naggeneen was indignant. He took a comically large leap to the top of The Patriarch, his tiny boots making no sound. “That pompous Higgins! He came down with a mind to board up my favourite nook! A man who thinks a ’28 port should be served at room temperature deserves all the corks he gets.”

“Your nook?” Justin leaned against a rack.

“Aye, behind the ’09 Château Margaux. A fine year, that. You should open a bottle soon.” Naggeneen patted the cask beneath him. “This old fellow, though. He and I have an understanding. I watch over him, he lets me have the odd sip to ensure he’s ageing properly. Symbiosis, your learned guests would call it.”

Justin smiled. “And the rearranging?”

“Art! Balance! That rack of Mosel was all wrong, too heavy on the left. I corrected it. And that Sauternes, languishing in the dark corner? Shameful. I gave it a place of honour. You should be thanking me.”

They argued it out like two barristers, over a bottle of remarkably good ’57 brandy that Naggeneen produced from a hiding place Justin swore hadn’t been there that morning.

Cluricaunes, Justin learned, were not like their cobbling cousins, the leprechauns. They were spirits of the wine cellar, born from the very breath of fermentation. A neglected cellar would drive one away; a cared-for one was a kingdom.

“I’ve been here since the first stone was laid,” Naggeneen said. “Saw your great-great-great-grandfather broach his first cask of poteen. A violent brew, that. I improved his stock, gently. A nudge here, a blending there. The Mac Carthy reputation was built, in part, on my palate.”

“And in return?” Justin asked.

“In return, I have a home. A drop of the finest to keep the damp from my bones. And peace. Until these interlopers with their dusters and their inventories come clattering about, treating the wine like lumber!”

“A truce, then.” Justin raised his brandy snifter. “The cellar is your domain. I shall instruct all future staff that the wine is solely my concern. They are not to touch, move, or taste a single drop without my express word. They may only fetch what I specifically request.”

Naggeneen eyed him suspiciously. “And you? Will you be clumping down here, disturbing the peace?”

“Only for consultation,” Justin said, grinning. “I suspect my education in oenology is sorely lacking. I may need a tutor.”

Naggeneen grinned back, wider. He swung the silver spigot off his shoulder and tapped it against Justin’s glass with a delicate *ping*. “A tutor, is it? Well now. For a suitable retainer, say, a thimbleful of The Patriarch on the first frost of each winter, and a promise of no more infernal gas lamps, they dry the air terribly, I might be persuaded.”

“The lamps stay,” Justin said. “But you shall have a cask of your own. The ’71 Shiraz.”

Naggeneen spat on his tiny hand and held it out. Justin, with solemn ceremony, wrapped his little finger around it.

From that night, the resignations ceased. O’Sullivan received one singular instruction: “The cellar is Mr. Mac Carthy’s private sanctuary. You enter only on his command, and you touch nothing. If you hear… laughter… pay it no mind.”
