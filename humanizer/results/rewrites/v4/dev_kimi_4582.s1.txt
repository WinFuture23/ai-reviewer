When Clara died, Nobody did not take her to the death cart.

He carried her upstream instead, past the broken floodwall, past the empty schoolhouse with its peeling paint, past the constable's office, where the jail stood empty because the prisoners had all sickened and been released to die at home. He walked until his boots touched the manicured gravel of the Manor House drive. Before the day was out there would be a filled grave under the garden wall where the lilacs grew, and at its head a stone: not a Bigwig stone, marked with lineage and claim, but a river stone, smooth and gray, marked with nothing but the passage of water.

"Thank you for your patience, Mr....?"

That was Reginald Bigwig, in one of the autumns before, the pause theatrical, the surname deliberately swallowed.

"Nobody, sir."

"Quite."

The name had stuck to his grandfather first and now clung to him like river-mist, convenient for those who preferred not to see the hands that sustained them. Millbrook knew him simply as Nobody. His first name was Elias; remember it, because the town didn't bother. For forty years he had lived with his ear tuned to the river, which gurgled over stone, whispered against the pilings of his dock and roared when the spring rains swelled its banks. He pulled silver fish from the current, mended the nets that fed his neighbors, and raised three children on the bank where the willows hung into the water. His labor was visible to anyone who looked. Few did.

Nobody met the Bigwigs only when necessity forced his hand. Each autumn, when the rains came, he trudged the muddy road to the Manor, hat in hand, to petition for the maintenance of the floodwall. Each autumn he waited in the antechamber while the family quarreled behind mahogany doors, their voices carrying through the keyhole like the screeching of jays over a carcass. Had you been standing next to him, you would have heard every word.

"It is *my* prerogative to set the dock fees," Reginald would shout.

"Your dock fees impoverish the merchants who pay *my* rents."

That was Old Man Bigwig, who measured his power in acres of disputed boundary lines.

"And neither of you considers the cost of the constabulary I must fund to keep order when your policies breed desperation!"

Cousin Millicent Bigwig, invariably slamming her ledger shut. She controlled the town's purse strings with the devotion of a miser counting phantom coins. Young Reginald collected tariffs the way boys collect stamps. The Bigwigs lived upstream, where the water ran cleaner, and governed from the Manor House as a family rather than an institution, passing decrees from father to son to cousin as casually as one might pass the salt.

They would come out hours later, faces flushed with the triumph of stalemate, and find Nobody standing quietly, his hat rotating in his work-worn hands. They looked through him. They addressed the wall above his head.

"The floodwall repairs will be deferred another season. The matter requires further study. The family is united in its decision to prioritize other concerns."

There would be an evening when he sat on his dock and thought that the work waiting for him tomorrow might be nets to mend, or perhaps a wall to build with his own hands where the Bigwigs' wall had failed.

He walked home through streets that grew shabbier with each passing year. The schoolhouse had once been bright with the sound of children reciting sums and scriptures. Its windows were boarded now; Cousin Millicent had diverted the teacher's salary to fund a new gate for the Manor. The children of Millbrook ran feral through the alleys instead, learning arithmetic by counting stolen apples and history from tavern songs that celebrated the Bigwigs' ancestors while cursing their own empty bellies.

Clara, his daughter, came home one evening with her knuckles bloody. A boy had tried to steal her river-smoothed stone, the treasure she used to practice letters in the mud, and she had fought him for it.

"He said reading is for Bigwigs," she told her father. She was upset. "He said we're only good for working and dying."

Nobody held her close and looked out at the water that carried away the waste of the town. The current had grown sluggish, choked with debris the Bigwigs had forbidden the council to dredge. The river was sick. There were fewer fish than there had been.

With no school to occupy them and no work to dignify them, the young men of Millbrook turned to the river, not for sustenance but for smuggling. They moved contraband past the Bigwigs' sleeping constables and spent their coins in the gin shops that proliferated where the mill had closed. Fights became common. Theft became casual.

A woman was beaten for a loaf of bread. Nobody carried her to the constable.

"The Bigwigs are currently debating the jurisdiction of domestic disputes," the constable told him. "I can't render aid until the cousin council reaches consensus."

"But she bleeds," Nobody said quietly.

"Then let her bleed until they decide."

He did not say it unkindly. He said it with the exhausted resignation of a man who had also learned that he was Nobody.

Winter came hard that year. At the market Nobody heard the first rumors of illness in the upstream villages. A fever, the gossip went. It starts with a cough. Then it wastes you, and strong men turn into phantoms.

He walked to the Manor once more, this time not for walls but for warning.

"The river carries more than fish," he told the servant who admitted him. "It carries what we put into it. The Bigwigs should know—there is sickness above. We must close the upstream intake. We must burn the refuse properly, not dump it where the current brings it back to us."

The servant looked at him with pity. "The family is in session, Mr. Nobody. They are discussing the inheritance of the north woodlot. It is a crisis of some urgency."

He left his warning written on a scrap of paper, weighted with a stone from his pocket. He never knew if they read it. He suspected they used it to steady a wobbling table leg.

Spring arrived with a false warmth, and the pestilence came with it, not as a storm but as a thief. It slipped into the hovels that clustered near the stagnant backwaters, where the Bigwigs' neglected drains emptied. The fever struck Clara first, then his son Thomas, then the neighbors' children, one by one, until the coughing filled the nights.

Nobody worked without rest. He ferried the sick across the river to the old herb-woman who lived on the far bank. He boiled water that he suspected had been poisoned by the very source of life he had trusted all his days. From the bank he watched the Bigwigs' carriage race past on the high road, bound for the country estates where the air was presumed pure, and the dust from the horses' hooves settled on the corpses being wrapped in shrouds down below.

In the worst week the death cart came daily. Nobody stood by the river, the ancient artery of the town's trade and life, and watched it carry disease out of the drains the Bigwigs had neglected. Hardly anyone in Millbrook had learned enough to recognize the danger in contaminated water. Upstream, the family argued on about precedence and profit. He counted what he himself had done for forty years: accepted the name they gave him, waited patiently in antechambers, believed that their squabbles were governance and their indifference was stewardship. He was grieving. The Bigwigs were still in session.

The gates of the Manor were locked, manned by guards in masks soaked with vinegar. But Nobody had worked the river long enough to know the secret paths. He came to the garden wall where the lilacs grew. Behind the windows the Bigwigs were still arguing; now the question was whether to quarantine the town or simply wait for the poor to stop inconveniencing them with their dying.

He dug a grave for his daughter there, in sight of those windows. He dug with his hands, because he had sold his spade to buy medicine that never came. He dug until the soil stained his fingers, and when the hole was deep enough he laid Clara down, wrapped in his best net, the one he had been saving for the day the fish returned in abundance.

A face appeared at a window. Millicent Bigwig, perhaps, or a servant.

"Move along! This is private property. I'll have the constable on you!"

Nobody stood. His clothes were ragged and his face gaunt, but for the first time in his life he did not rotate his hat in his hands. He let it fall to the grass. He looked up at the window and spoke in a voice that carried over the garden wall, clear and cold as the river in winter.

"I am not Nobody," he said. "I am Elias. I am the man who mends your world while you break it. I am the father who buried wisdom because you stole its schoolhouse. I am the man who understands, now, that your chaos is not accident but appetite. You consume and you quarrel, and we die of your indigestion."

The face withdrew, and the argument inside resumed, quieter now, perhaps, or simply drowned by the wind. Elias walked home past the dying and the dead, past the stagnant pools where the mosquitoes rose, and sat on his dock as the sun set, dipping his hands into the current. His sorrow was heavy, and it was his. Inside, his son Thomas slept fitfully, weak but alive. The water ran dark, carrying the ashes of the burning shacks downstream, away from the Bigwigs' sight.
