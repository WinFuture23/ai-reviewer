„Alright“, Justin Mac Carthy called, and his boots echoed on the stone. „The game's up. Show yourself. You've terrorised my staff and pilfered my best stock. I'll have a word.“

In front of him stood the oldest cask in the cellar, a pre-Phylloxera claret he called The Patriarch. On the floor before it was a tiny, perfect puddle of ruby wine. Beside the puddle lay a thimble. Justin was annoyed and, in equal measure, amazed.

Nothing answered. Then, from directly behind a barrel of amontillado, a voice like dry leaves and creaking wood replied: „Terrorised, is it? And pilfered? A harsh tongue for a Mac Carthy, and you a man famed for his hospitality.“

The letter was upstairs in the library at Ballinacarthy, on the polished oak desk: the fifth letter of resignation in as many months. This one came from a butler named Higgins, whose nerves, he had claimed, had been forged to steel in the service of a Bengal Lancer.

„*…and whilst I have the utmost respect for your person and your household, sir,*“ Justin read aloud over the crackle of the applewood fire, „*the persistent and…* *aethereal*… *interference in the wine cellar makes the execution of my duties quite impossible. I regret to inform you that I shall be departing at first light. Yours, etc.*“

He read it in a rich baritone, and he was exasperated. He sighed. Picture him, if you will: a squire in his prime, known across three counties of Cork and beyond for his open door, his roaring fires and, above all, his claret. The Mac Carthy cellars were a cool, vaulted labyrinth of stone beneath the house, and there were vintages down there that could make a Frenchman weep.

Casks were being found tapped, not hacked open, but pierced with a neat, drilled hole. The finest bottles left their designated bins and turned up rearranged in puzzling, almost artistic patterns. A ’45 Lafite sat perched atop a barrel of porter like a ruby crown. No groaning spectres, no clanking chains; only, now and then, the faint, echoing sound of a satisfied chuckle and the rustle of tiny, quick feet.

His butlers, proud and unflappable professionals every one, went down resolved and came back up frightened and ashamed.

„By my own coattails, sir“, one of them swore. „Pinned to the rack of ’63 Burgundy.“

„Corks“, said another. „A whole battalion of them. Like tiny cannonballs.“

Higgins, the last, had simply mumbled about „a little man with a face like a wrinkled walnut and an impudent grin“, and packed his bags.

Justin poured himself a glass of whisky, not from the cellar but from the decanter on the sideboard. „Enough“, he said to the empty room. He would beard this lion (or this leprechaun) in its den.

At his table, guests would marvel. „No, not the ’65“, he would tell them. „The ’66 is singing tonight, I can feel it in the stones.“ And he would be proven right, again and again, in ways that bordered on the mystical. His wine pairings were uncanny, his intuition for when a bottle stood at its peak became legendary, and his hospitality more famed than ever.

He took no candle. The cellar was equipped with modern gas lamps, and their hiss was a familiar whisper in the stillness as he unlocked the heavy iron-bound door and went down the worn stone steps. They cast a soft, yellow glow over the ranks of barrels and the silent battalions of bottles, their shoulders dusty with time. For a long moment he stood and listened. The drip of a distant tap. The house settling. Perhaps, he thought, it was all fancy, a collective madness.

Justin spun.

Perched on the spigot of the amontillado barrel was a figure no taller than his boot, in a coat of faded green and breeches of brown leather, with a hat that might once have been pointed and was now crumpled like a forgotten mushroom. Higgins had been right about the face: a wrinkled walnut, lit by two eyes that sparkled with a millennium of cunning. Slung over his shoulder like a soldier's rifle was a tiny, finely wrought silver spigot.

„Naggeneen“, the little man said, tipping his hat. „Cluricaune of Ballinacarthy Cellars, at your service. Though it seems my service – keeping your barrels sound and your wine from turning to vinegar – isn't appreciated.“

To his own surprise, Justin made a slight bow. „Justin Mac Carthy. And I appreciate sound wine well enough. It's the midnight tastings and the assault on my staff I could do without.“

„Assault!“ Naggeneen squeaked, indignant, and took a comically large leap to the top of The Patriarch. His tiny boots made no sound. „That pompous Higgins! He came down with a mind to board up my favourite nook! A man who thinks a ’28 port should be served at room temperature deserves all the corks he gets.“

„Your nook?“ Justin leaned against a rack.

„Aye, behind the ’09 Château Margaux. A fine year, that. You should open a bottle soon.“ Naggeneen patted the huge cask beneath him. „This old fellow, though. He and I have an understanding. I watch over him, he lets me have the odd sip to ensure he's ageing properly. Symbiosis, your learned guests would call it.“

„And in return?“

„In return, I have a home. A drop of the finest to keep the damp from my bones. And peace. Until these interlopers with their dusters and their inventories come clattering about, treating the wine like lumber!“

Naggeneen would point out a cask with the faintest whisper of taint, one no one but him could detect, and save Justin a fortune. Justin would bring down each new acquisition and listen intently while the Cluricaune delivered his judgment with the withering authority of a Parisian wine critic. At such times the arch grin softened, and Naggeneen was something close to content.

Justin was amused. „And the rearranging?“

„Art!“ declared the Cluricaune. „Balance! That rack of Mosel was all wrong, too heavy on the left. I corrected it. And that Sauternes, languishing in the dark corner? Shameful. I gave it a place of honour. You should be thanking me.“

One winter's night they shared a thimble of The Patriarch by the light of a single, guttering candle, the gas lamp politely off. Naggeneen looked up at the great, shadowy vaults.

„You know“, he said, his leaf-voice quiet, „they think we're all gone. The Cluricaunes. That men like you, with your ledgers and your science, have no room for us anymore.“

Justin swirled the ancient wine. „Then they've never tasted a wine cared for by one. Ballinacarthy has room, Naggeneen. For as long as the vines grow and the barrels age.“

The Cluricaune nodded, took a minuscule sip and lifted his thimble. „To the wine.“

„To the guardian of the wine“, Justin replied, and two glasses met in the dark, one large, one small.

They debated like two barristers, over a bottle of remarkably good ’57 brandy that Naggeneen produced from a hiding place Justin swore hadn't been there that morning.

„We're not leprechauns, mind“, Naggeneen said. „Cobblers, the lot of them, cousins or no. We're spirits of the wine cellar, born from the very breath of fermentation. Neglect a cellar and you'll drive one of us away. Care for it, and it's a kingdom.“

„And you're its king.“

„A king who takes his duties seriously.“

„A truce, then“, Justin proposed, raising his brandy snifter. „The cellar is your domain. I shall instruct all future staff that the wine is solely my concern. They are not to touch, move, or taste a single drop without my express word. They may only fetch what I specifically request.“

Naggeneen eyed him suspiciously. „And you? Will you be clumping down here, disturbing the peace?“

„Only for consultation“, Justin said, grinning. „I suspect my education in oenology is sorely lacking. I may need a tutor.“

The arch grin came back, wider than ever. Naggeneen swung the silver spigot off his shoulder and tapped it against Justin's glass with a delicate *ping*. „A tutor, is it? Well now.“ His voice went softer. „I've been here since the first stone was laid. Saw your great-great-great-grandfather broach his first cask of poteen. A violent brew, that. I improved his stock, gently. A nudge here, a blending there. The Mac Carthy reputation was built, in part, on my palate.“

He let that stand for a moment. „For a suitable retainer – say, a thimbleful of The Patriarch on the first frost of each winter, and a promise of no more infernal gas lamps, they dry the air terribly – I might be persuaded.“

„The lamps stay“, Justin said firmly. „But you shall have a cask of your own. The ’71 Shiraz. It's bold enough to suit you.“

Naggeneen was delighted. He spat on his tiny hand and held it out, and Justin, with solemn ceremony, wrapped his own little finger around it. The pact was sealed.

The resignations ceased. The new butler, a quiet man named O'Sullivan, received one singular, strange instruction: „The cellar is Mr. Mac Carthy's private sanctuary. You enter only on his command, and you touch nothing. If you hear… laughter… pay it no mind.“ And if O'Sullivan sometimes saw, from the corner of his eye, the master sitting on an upturned crate deep in the vaults, seemingly talking to a barrel, or if he occasionally found two glasses set out where only one had been fetched, he was far too well-trained to mention it.
