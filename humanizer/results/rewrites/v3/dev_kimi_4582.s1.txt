Nobody carried the woman to the constable himself. She had been beaten for a loaf of bread.

"The Bigwigs are currently debating the jurisdiction of domestic disputes," the constable told him. "No aid can be rendered. Not until the cousin council reaches consensus."

"But she bleeds," Nobody said quietly.

"Then let her bleed until they decide."

The constable did not say it unkindly. He was exhausted, and he had given up.

Nobody met the Bigwigs only when necessity forced his hand. Each autumn, when the rains came, he trudged the muddy road to the Manor House, hat in hand, to petition for the maintenance of the floodwall. Each autumn he waited in the antechamber while the family quarreled behind mahogany doors, their voices carrying through the keyhole like the screeching of jays over a carcass.

"It is *my* prerogative to set the dock fees," Reginald would shout.

"Your dock fees impoverish the merchants who pay *my* rents," Old Man Bigwig would retort.

"And neither of you considers the cost of the constabulary I must fund to keep order when your policies breed desperation," Millicent would cry. Then she would slam her ledger shut. She always slammed it.

The Bigwigs lived upstream, where the water ran cleaner. They governed from the Manor House, a family rather than an institution, passing decrees from father to son to cousin as casually as one might pass the salt. There was Old Man Bigwig, who measured his power in acres of disputed boundary lines; Young Reginald Bigwig, who collected tariffs the way boys (perhaps you were one) collect stamps; and Cousin Millicent Bigwig, who controlled the town's purse strings with the devotion of a miser counting phantom coins.

They would emerge hours later, pleased with their stalemate, to find Nobody standing quietly, turning his hat in his work-worn hands. They looked through him. One of them would address the wall above his head.

"The floodwall repairs will be deferred another season. The matter requires further study. The family is united in its decision to prioritize other concerns."

"Thank you for your patience, Mr...?" Reginald would say, the pause theatrical, the surname deliberately swallowed.

"Nobody, sir."

"Quite."

The name had stuck to his grandfather first, and it had stuck to him. The town of Millbrook knew him by nothing else. For forty years he had lived on the river with his ear tuned to its voices, the gurgling over stone, the whispering against the pilings of his dock, the roaring when spring rains swelled its banks. He understood all of them. He pulled silver fish from the current, mended the nets that fed his neighbors, and raised three children on the bank where the willows leaned into the water.

He walked home from the Manor through streets that grew shabbier with each passing year. The schoolhouse, once bright with the sound of children reciting sums and scriptures, stood empty, its windows boarded after Cousin Millicent had diverted the teacher's salary to fund a new gate for the Manor. The children of Millbrook ran feral through the alleys instead, learning arithmetic by counting stolen apples and history from tavern songs that celebrated the Bigwigs' ancestors while cursing their own empty bellies.

Clara came home one evening with bloody knuckles.

"He said reading is for Bigwigs," she told her father. "He said we're only good for working and dying."

The boy had tried to steal her stone, a river-smoothed one she used to practice her letters in the mud. She had fought him for it, and she was upset. Nobody held her close.

He looked out at the water afterwards. The current had grown sluggish, choked with debris the Bigwigs had forbidden the council to dredge. The river carried away the waste of the town, and the fish were fewer.

No school, no work. The young men of Millbrook turned to the river, not for sustenance but for smuggling, and moved their contraband past the Bigwigs' sleeping constables. They spent their coins in the gin shops that multiplied where the mill had closed. Fights became common. Theft became casual.

Winter came hard that year. At the market Nobody heard the first rumors of illness in the upstream villages: a fever that started with a cough, a wasting sickness that turned strong men into phantoms. He walked to the Manor once more, and this time not for walls.

"The river carries more than fish," he told the servant who admitted him. "It carries what we put into it. The Bigwigs should know—there is sickness above. We must close the upstream intake. We must burn the refuse properly, not dump it where the current brings it back to us."

The servant looked at him with pity. "The family is in session, Mr. Nobody. They are discussing the inheritance of the north woodlot. It is a crisis of some urgency."

He left his warning on a scrap of paper, weighted with a stone from his pocket. He never knew if they read it. He suspected, and you may too, that they used it to steady a wobbling table leg.

The pestilence came in spring. It slipped into the hovels that clustered near the stagnant backwaters, where the Bigwigs' neglected drains emptied. The fever struck Clara first, then his son Thomas, then the neighbors' children, one by one, until the coughing filled the nights.

Nobody worked without rest. He ferried the sick across the river to the old herb-woman who lived on the far bank. He boiled water that he suspected was poisoned by the very source he had trusted all his days. He sold his spade to buy medicine. The medicine never came. From the bank he watched the Bigwigs' carriage race past on the high road, bound for the country estates where the air was presumed pure, and the horses' hooves threw up dust that settled on the corpses being wrapped in shrouds below.

He came home past the dying and the dead, past the stagnant pools where the mosquitoes rose, and sat on his dock as the sun set, dipping his hands into the current. The water ran dark. It carried the ashes of the burning shacks downstream, away from the Bigwigs' sight. Inside, Thomas slept fitfully, weak but alive. There was work to do tomorrow: nets to mend, perhaps, or a wall to build with his own hands where the Bigwigs' wall had failed.

In the worst week the death cart came daily. Nobody stood by the river.

When Clara died, he did not take her to the death cart. He carried her upstream, past the broken floodwall, past the empty schoolhouse with its peeling paint, past the constable's office, where the jail stood empty because the prisoners had all sickened and been released to die at home. He walked until his boots touched the manicured gravel of the Manor House drive.

The gates were locked, manned by guards in masks soaked with vinegar. But Nobody had worked the river long enough to know the secret paths. He came to the garden wall where the lilacs grew, and there, in sight of the windows behind which the Bigwigs were still arguing—now about whether to quarantine the town or simply wait for the poor to stop inconveniencing them with their dying—he dug a grave for his daughter.

He dug with his hands. He dug until the soil stained his fingers, and when the hole was deep enough, he laid Clara down, wrapped in his best net, the one he had been saving for the day the fish returned in abundance.

A face appeared at a window—Millicent Bigwig, perhaps, or a servant.

"Move along! This is private property. I'll have the constable on you!"

Nobody stood. His clothes were ragged, his face gaunt, and for the first time in his life he did not turn his hat in his hands. He let it fall to the grass. He looked up at the window and spoke in a voice that carried over the garden wall.

"I am not Nobody," he said. "I am Elias. I am the man who mends your world while you break it. I am the father who buried wisdom because you stole its schoolhouse. I am the man who understands, now, that your chaos is not accident but appetite. You consume and you quarrel, and we die of your indigestion."

He filled the grave and placed a stone at its head, not a Bigwig stone, marked with lineage and claim, but a river stone, smooth and gray, marked with nothing but the passage of water. By then the face had withdrawn from the window. Inside, the argument had resumed, quieter now, perhaps, or simply drowned by the wind.
