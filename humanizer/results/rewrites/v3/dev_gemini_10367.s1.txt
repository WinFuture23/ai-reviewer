"She is quite dedicated, is she not?"

Mr. Polleto was standing at the end of the corridor. He held no candle, yet I could see him by a faint, sickly luminescence that clung to his skin.

"You did this," I said. I had fallen against the wall, and where my hand touched the stone, the masonry was soft, like rotting fruit. "You let them bury her."

"I did not suggest it." He stepped closer, and with every step he took, the floorboards beneath him shriveled. "I merely facilitated the equilibrium. I am a catalyst, Frederick. I accelerate what is already there. The town held a secret rot; I simply gave it the agency to manifest."

He reached out a hand. His fingers were elongated, the tips fading into a grey mist. I was terrified.

"The trees were the first to go," he continued, and his voice had become a chorus of dry whispers. "They are simple structures. Humans are more complex. They take longer to turn to dust. But look at your hands, Frederick. Look at the edges of your life."

I looked down. My fingernails were translucent. The skin of my wrists was beginning to flake away into a fine, grey powder.

* * *

The fire in my study has burned down to a heap of embers. They tell me that Steepleford is no longer on the maps, that the postal carriages bypass the valley entirely and the heavy mists have reclaimed the geography of the place. For forty years I have tried to bury all of it beneath the soot of London. I am an old man now, of a lineage that once valued logic and the hard certainties of the law, and I am afraid as I take up the pen. (You will forgive me if I do not tell it in order.)

I must record the truth of Salter's Lane, and of the man, if he can be called such, named Mr. Polleto. If I do not, the sickness that started in that soil may one day find its way into the wider world, and humanity is ill-equipped for a blight that consumes not the flesh but the very essence of form.

### The Boar's Head

"I'll go no further than the Boar's Head, sir."

The carriage driver said it at the edge of the village green, his eyes going to the rising slope of Salter's Lane. "The air up there... it doesn't sit right in the lungs."

It was the autumn of 1882. I was a young solicitor then, sent to settle the accounts of the Hawkins estate. Steepleford lay tucked into a fold of the hills, and it was silent, not with the quiet of the countryside but with a heavy, pressing stillness. I dismissed the driver's superstitions, took my briefcase, and walked the remaining mile toward the Hawkins manor.

The trees along Salter's Lane were ancient oaks and elms, and in that season they should have been gold. They were a uniform grey. Nor were they dead, not in any sense I understood. A light breeze stirred as I passed, and a branch as thick as your thigh neither broke nor swayed. It dissolved. It crumbled into a fine, microscopic powder that hung in the air like a veil of ash.

I reached out to touch the trunk of a great oak, and my finger passed through the bark as if it were made of compressed smoke. There was no wood beneath, only a desiccated, calcified dust.

By the time I reached the manor, my black coat had turned grey.

* * *

Every morning I sweep a fine, grey dust from my pillow. My doctors call it a skin condition, the remnant of some "valley fever" I contracted in my youth.

* * *

"Mr. Frederick."

The voice did not seem to come from the throat of the man on the porch. It vibrated in the air around him. "You have come for the ledgers. But the ledgers are written in a language of loss that you may find difficult to balance."

This was Mr. Polleto, the "caretaker" hired by the distant cousins of the Hawkins line. He was tall, in a suit of such deep charcoal that it seemed to absorb the dim light. His skin had the quality of wet parchment. His eyes were too large for his face, and they shimmered, gelatinous and unblinking.

I was afraid of him from the first moment, and disgusted by him. The grass at his feet was not merely dead. It was translucent, the blades turned into a clear, brittle glass, and they shattered under the weight of his shadow.

"The town is unwell, Mr. Polleto," I said. I was clutching my briefcase.

"The town is transitioning." He smiled, thinly, without lips. "Change is often mistaken for malady by those who cling to the old shapes."

In the foyer hung the portrait of a woman, beautiful and spirited, who had supposedly fled to the continent years before. Inside, the air was worse. Polleto showed me to a room on the second floor, and on the way we passed through the center of the house, where a large section of the hallway had been bricked over, crudely, violently. The mortar was fresh. The bricks looked ancient, as if they were aging centuries in a matter of days.

That night the "Steepleford Cough" took hold of me. It was the same sound I had heard from the cottages in the village, dry and rattling, as if the villagers were breathing in ground glass.

I could not sleep. The house groaned, but not with the settling of timber; it sounded like a moan. From behind the bricked-up wall came a rhythmic thudding. I took my candle and went out into the hallway.

* * *

I do not know how I found the strength to move. I lunged past Polleto. He did not try to stop me. He watched with those unblinking eyes, as if my escape were a temporary delay in an inevitable process.

The stairs crumbled like sandbags under my boots. I burst out of the front door and into Salter's Lane. The moon was out. The trees were no longer recognizable as trees; they were pillars of ash, held together only by the lack of wind, and as I ran, the air my body displaced was enough. The oaks collapsed in my wake. A hundred-year-old grove vanished behind me into a cloud of grey soot, a silent avalanche of dust.

In the village the lights were dim. At a window I saw a woman whose features were blurring, her nose and chin softening into a featureless mask of grey skin.

The carriage driver was on the green, slumped over his seat. He was dead, but he was not a corpse in the way I understood the word. He was a hollow shell, his clothes draped over a pile of fine white powder.

I grabbed the reins and whipped the horses. They were terrified; their hooves struck the ground with a muffled, hollow sound. We drove through the mist and through the falling ash until the air began to bite with the cold of a winter frost.

* * *

I never went back. I resigned from the firm and moved to the heart of London, for the density of its stone and the permanence of its iron.

The elders of Steepleford had bricked her in alive, perhaps Polleto himself had, for some perceived transgression. I know that she is still staring, somewhere in a place that no longer exists on any map, behind a wall of weeping brick, her eyes fixed on the horizon, unmaking the world. She stared through the house and through the hills at the trees until they forgot how to be wood, and at the people of Steepleford until their lungs forgot how to hold air. And I know that Mr. Polleto is walking, toward the cities, toward the crowds, carrying with him the silence of Salter's Lane.

Sometimes, when the room is very quiet, I hear the sound of a brick being tapped from the inside. The walls of my study are remarkably thin.

### A single missing brick

Near the eye level of a tall woman, one brick was missing from the masonry. I put my eye to the gap.

I had expected darkness, or a hidden room. There was a pair of eyes. They were the color of clouded amber, and they glowed with a fierce spite. They did not blink. The woman behind the wall did not scream. She simply looked.

They were the eyes of Amber Maria Hawkins, the woman from the portrait in the foyer. She had not fled to the continent.

Where her gaze landed on the opposite wall, the wallpaper blistered and turned to grey ash.

"Amber Maria provides the will," said Mr. Polleto. "I provide the medium."
