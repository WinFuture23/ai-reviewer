Every morning I sweep a fine grey dust from my pillow. My doctors call it a skin condition, the remnant of some "valley fever" I contracted in my youth. I let them call it that. It is easier than telling them about Salter's Lane.

You will not find Steepleford on a map any longer. The postal carriages bypass the valley entirely, and the heavy mists have taken back what was there. So they tell me; I have not gone to look.

"I'll go no further than the 'Boar's Head,' sir."

The carriage driver said it with his eyes on the rising slope of Salter's Lane, and he would not take me a yard past the village green. "The air up there... it doesn't sit right in the lungs."

I was a young solicitor, and I thought him a superstitious fool. I walked the remaining mile to the Hawkins manor on foot. The village lay tucked into a fold of the hills, and it was silent: not the peaceful quiet of the countryside, but a heavy, pressurized stillness. It was the first thing I noticed, and I paid it no attention.

The trees along the lane were oaks and elms, ancient ones, and in autumn they ought to have been gold. They were a uniform, ghostly grey. They were not dead, or not in any sense I knew. A light breeze came up as I passed, and a branch as thick as a man's thigh did not break and did not sway. It dissolved. It came apart into a fine powder that hung in the air like a veil of ash.

I put my hand to the trunk of a great oak, and my finger went through the bark as though it were compressed smoke. There was no wood behind it, only a desiccated, calcified dust. Some of it got into my mouth. It tasted of copper and old pennies.

My coat was black when I left the Boar's Head. By the time I reached the manor it was grey.

I saw the driver once more before that night was out. He was on the village green, slumped over his seat, and he was dead, though not a corpse in the way I understood the word. He was a hollow shell. His clothes were draped over a pile of fine white powder.

### "You have come for the ledgers"

"Mr. Frederick."

The voice did not seem to come from a throat. It vibrated out of the air around him.

"You have come for the ledgers. But the ledgers are written in a language of loss that you may find difficult to balance."

He was standing on the porch. He was tall, dressed in a suit of so deep a charcoal that it seemed to absorb what little light there was. His skin had the quality of wet parchment. His eyes were too large for his face; they shimmered, gelatinous, and they did not blink. Looking at him made me dizzy. Then came a sudden, violent nausea, as though my own cells were recoiling from being near him. The grass at his feet was not merely dead. It had gone translucent, the blades turned to clear, brittle glass, and it shattered under the weight of his shadow.

"The town is unwell, Mr. Polleto," I managed to say. I was clutching my briefcase.

"The town is transitioning." He smiled, thinly; he had almost no lips. "Change is often mistaken for malady by those who cling to the old shapes."

He was, on paper, the caretaker, hired by distant cousins of the Hawkins line. More than that nobody told me.

Inside, the air was worse. It smelled of sun-bleached bone. He showed me to a room on the second floor, and on the way, in the center of the house, we passed a section of hallway that had been bricked over, crudely, violently. The mortar was fresh. The bricks looked ancient, as if they were aging centuries in a matter of days.

### Ground glass

That night I began to cough. It was the same sound I had heard from the cottages in the village, dry and rattling, as though the people inside were breathing in ground glass. In Steepleford they called it the Steepleford Cough.

I could not sleep. The house groaned, and not the way timber settles; it was nearer to a moan. Under it, from behind the bricked-up wall, came a rhythmic thudding. I took my candle and went into the hallway.

There was a gap in the masonry, a single missing brick, at about the eye-level of a tall woman. I expected darkness behind it, or a hidden room.

I peered through. There was a pair of eyes.

I knew them from the portrait in the foyer: a beautiful, spirited woman who had supposedly fled to the continent years before. Amber Maria Hawkins. It was the accounts of the Hawkins estate I had come to settle, in the autumn of 1882. She had not fled anywhere. She had been entombed.

Her eyes were no longer human. They were the color of clouded amber, and they glowed with a fierce, radioactive spite. She did not blink. She did not scream. She looked. Where her gaze landed on the opposite wall, the wallpaper blistered and turned to grey ash.

The town elders had bricked her in alive, for some transgression, real or only perceived; perhaps Polleto had done it with them. I believed that night, and I believe now, that the blight on Salter's Lane was never a disease of the soil. It was her. She was looking through the house, through the hills, at the trees until they forgot how to be wood, and at the people of Steepleford until their lungs forgot how to hold air.

"She is quite dedicated, is she not?"

Polleto stood at the end of the corridor. He held no candle; a faint, sickly luminescence clung to his skin, enough to see him by.

"You did this," I gasped. I had fallen against the wall, and where my hand touched it the stone was soft, like rotting fruit. "You let them bury her."

"I did not suggest it." He stepped closer, and with every step the floorboards shriveled beneath him. "I merely facilitated the equilibrium. I am a catalyst, Frederick. I accelerate what is already there. The town held a secret rot; I simply gave it the agency to manifest. Amber Maria provides the will; I provide the medium."

He reached out a hand. The fingers were elongated, their tips fading into a grey mist. I was more afraid than I have been before or since. He was not a man, nor even a ghost. The nearest word I have found for him in forty years is entropy: the end of things, in a charcoal suit.

"The trees were the first to go," he went on. His voice had become a chorus of dry whispers. "They are simple structures. Humans are more complex. They take longer to turn to dust. But look at your hands, Frederick. Look at the edges of your life."

I looked down. My fingernails were translucent. The skin at my wrists was flaking away into the same fine grey powder.

I do not know where I found the strength. I lunged past him. He did not move to stop me; he watched with those unblinking eyes, as if my escape were a temporary delay in something that would happen regardless. The stairs crumbled like sandbags under my boots, and I burst out of the front door into Salter's Lane.

The moon was out. The trees were no longer recognizable as trees. They were pillars of ash, held together only by the lack of wind, and as I ran, the air my body pushed aside was enough to bring the oaks down in my wake. A hundred-year-old grove went to grey soot as I passed, a silent, thundering avalanche of dust.

In the village the lights were dim. At one window I saw a woman whose features were blurring, her nose and chin softening into a featureless mask of grey skin. So that was the illness of Steepleford, not the cough alone: the self, coming apart slowly.

On the green I found the driver as I have described him. I grabbed the reins and whipped the horses. They were terrified, and their hooves struck the ground with a muffled, hollow sound. We drove through the mist and the falling ash until the air began to bite with the cold, honest sting of a winter frost.

### Valley fever

I never went back. I resigned from the firm and moved to the heart of London, for the density of its stone and the permanence of its iron.

My name is Frederick. I come of a lineage that once valued logic and the hard certainties of the law. I am an old man now; my hands tremble when I take up the pen, and the fire in my study has burned down to embers while I write. For forty years I have tried to bury all this beneath the soot of London. I am writing it down because the sickness that started in that soil may one day find its way into the wider world, and humanity is ill-equipped for a blight that consumes not the flesh, but form itself.

I am not whole. The blight is not gone; it is merely patient. Amber Maria is still staring, I am sure of it, somewhere that exists on no map, behind a wall of weeping brick, her eyes fixed on the horizon, unmaking the world. And Mr. Polleto is walking toward the cities, toward the crowds, carrying the silence of Salter's Lane with him: the guest who never leaves, the shadow that dissolves the floor, the man who watches while the trees turn to dust. In the end, we are all just dust waiting for the wind. You may find that easier to believe than I once did.

The walls of my study have begun to feel remarkably thin.

Sometimes, when the room is very quiet, I hear the sound of a brick being tapped from the inside.
