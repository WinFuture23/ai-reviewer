He dug with his hands, because he had sold his spade to buy medicine that never came.

The ground at the foot of the Manor's garden wall was soft, and it stained his fingers. Lilacs grew there. Above him were the windows behind which the Bigwigs were arguing. That week the question was whether to quarantine the town or simply wait for the poor to stop inconveniencing them with their dying. Around the front the gates were locked, and the guards wore masks soaked in vinegar. He had not come by the gates. He had worked the river long enough to know the secret paths.

"Thank you for your patience, Mr...?"

"Nobody, sir."

"Quite."

That was Young Reginald Bigwig, some autumn years before. He made the pause before the surname theatrical and swallowed the surname on purpose. Every autumn went that way. When the rains came, Elias trudged the muddy road up to the Manor, hat in hand, to petition for the maintenance of the floodwall. Then he waited in the antechamber while the family quarreled behind mahogany doors. You could hear every word through the keyhole.

"It is *my* prerogative to set the dock fees!"

"Your dock fees impoverish the merchants who pay *my* rents."

"And neither of you considers the cost of the constabulary I must fund to keep order when your policies breed desperation!" Then, invariably, a ledger slammed shut.

The first voice was Reginald's. He collected tariffs the way boys collect stamps. The second belonged to Old Man Bigwig, who measured his power in acres of disputed boundary lines. The ledger was Cousin Millicent's. She controlled the town's purse strings and guarded them like a miser counting phantom coins.

Hours later they came out, flushed with the triumph of stalemate, and found him standing there, his hat turning in his work-worn hands. They addressed the wall above his head. The floodwall repairs would be deferred another season. The matter required further study. The family was united in its decision to prioritize other concerns.

The Manor House was not an institution. It was a family, and its decrees went from father to son to cousin as casually as one passes the salt. The Bigwigs lived upstream, where the water ran cleaner. Downstream, Elias had lived with the river for forty years. He pulled silver fish from the current, mended the nets that fed his neighbors, and raised three children on the bank under the willows. Millbrook called him Nobody. The name had stuck to his grandfather first and had come down to him. It was convenient for those who preferred not to see the hands that sustained them.

On the evening after the grave he sat on his dock at sunset with his hands in the current. The water ran dark. It carried the ashes of the burning shacks downstream, away from the Bigwigs' sight. He had walked home past the dying and the dead, past the stagnant pools where the mosquitoes rose. His son Thomas was inside, weak but alive, sleeping fitfully. There was work to do tomorrow: nets to mend, perhaps, or a wall to build with his own hands where the Bigwigs' wall had failed. His sorrow was heavy, and it was his, and it would not let him rest.

After the autumn petitions he always walked home through streets that grew shabbier each year. The schoolhouse had once been loud with children reciting sums and scriptures. Now it stood empty with its windows boarded. Cousin Millicent had diverted the teacher's salary to pay for a new gate for the Manor. The children of Millbrook ran feral through the alleys. They learned arithmetic by counting stolen apples. They learned history from tavern songs that celebrated the Bigwigs' ancestors and cursed their own empty bellies.

One evening his daughter Clara came home with bloody knuckles. A boy had tried to take her river-smoothed stone, the one she used to practice her letters in the mud, and she had fought him.

"He said reading is for Bigwigs," she told her father. She was close to tears. "He said we're only good for working and dying."

Elias held her close. That was the first time he was afraid, though he could not yet have said of what. Out on the water the current had gone sluggish, choked with debris the Bigwigs had forbidden the council to dredge. The river was sick. The fish were fewer.

There was no school to occupy the young men of Millbrook and no work to give them dignity. They went to the river for smuggling instead of for food. They moved contraband past the Bigwigs' sleeping constables and spent their coins in the gin shops that had multiplied where the mill closed. Fights became common. Theft became casual. A woman was beaten for a loaf of bread. Elias carried her to the constable, who told him that the Bigwigs were currently debating the jurisdiction of domestic disputes. No aid could be rendered until the cousin council reached consensus.

"But she bleeds," Elias said quietly.

"Then let her bleed until they decide."

The constable said it wearily, and not unkindly. He too had learned that, up at the Manor, he was Nobody.

The winter was hard that year. At the market Elias heard the first rumors of illness in the upstream villages: a fever that started with a cough, a wasting sickness that turned strong men into phantoms. He went to the Manor once more. This time he did not come about walls.

"The river carries more than fish," he told the servant who let him in. "It carries what we put into it. The Bigwigs should know. There is sickness above. We must close the upstream intake. We must burn the refuse properly, not dump it where the current brings it back to us."

The servant pitied him. "The family is in session, Mr. Nobody. They are discussing the inheritance of the north woodlot. It is a crisis of some urgency."

In case you are wondering about the warning, it ended up on a scrap of paper, weighted with a stone from his pocket. Whether anyone read it, he never learned. He suspected they used it to steady a wobbling table leg.

In spring the pestilence reached the town. It slipped into the hovels that clustered near the stagnant backwaters, where the Bigwigs' neglected drains emptied. The fever struck Clara first, then Thomas, then the neighbors' children, one after another, until the coughing filled the nights.

Elias worked without rest. He ferried the sick across the river to the old herb-woman on the far bank. He boiled water that he suspected was poisoned, the same water he had trusted all his days. He watched the Bigwigs' carriage race past on the high road, bound for the country estates where the air was presumed pure. The horses threw up dust, and it settled on the corpses being wrapped in shrouds below.

In the worst week the death cart came daily. Nobody in the lower town had known to fear the water, because nobody there had been taught to. Up at the Manor they were still arguing precedence and profit. Elias stood by the river and was ashamed. He was ashamed of the hat turning in his hands, of the antechambers, of every autumn he had answered to the name they gave him and taken their squabbles for governance.

When Clara died, he did not take her to the death cart. He carried her upstream, past the broken floodwall, past the empty schoolhouse with its peeling paint, past the constable's office. The jail there stood empty. The prisoners had all sickened and been released to die at home. He walked until his boots touched the manicured gravel of the Manor House drive. From there he took the old path to the garden wall.

When the hole was deep enough he laid her down, wrapped in his best net. He had been saving it for the day the fish returned in abundance.

A face appeared at a window. It may have been Millicent Bigwig, or it may have been a servant. From the garden, no one could have said. A voice called down, demanding that he move along. This was private property. The constable would be summoned.

Elias stood up. His clothes were ragged and his face gaunt. For the first time in his life he did not turn his hat in his hands. He let it fall to the grass, looked up at the window, and spoke loudly enough to carry over the garden wall.

"I am not Nobody," he said. "I am Elias. I am the man who mends your world while you break it. I am the father who buried wisdom because you stole its schoolhouse. I am the man who understands, now, that your chaos is not accident but appetite. You consume and you quarrel, and we die of your indigestion."

The face withdrew. Inside, the argument resumed. It was quieter now, perhaps, or the wind drowned it out.

He filled the grave. At its head he placed a stone. It was not a Bigwig stone, carved with lineage and claim. It was a river stone, smooth and gray, marked with nothing but the passage of water.
