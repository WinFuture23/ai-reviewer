The instruction O'Sullivan received on his first day at Ballinacarthy was a single one, and a strange one: "The cellar is Mr. Mac Carthy's private sanctuary. You enter only on his command, and you touch nothing. If you hear… laughter… pay it no mind."

O'Sullivan was a quiet man, and the new butler, and he asked no questions. The butlers before him had not lasted.

"*…and whilst I have the utmost respect for your person and your household, sir,*" Justin Mac Carthy had read aloud in the library, in his rich baritone, "*the persistent and…* *aethereal*… *interference in the wine cellar makes the execution of my duties quite impossible. I regret to inform you that I shall be departing at first light. Yours, etc.*"

The letter lay on the polished oak desk. It was signed Higgins, a man who had claimed nerves of steel forged in the service of a Bengal Lancer, and it was the fifth letter of resignation in as many months. Justin sighed over it by the applewood fire. He was exasperated, and he was worried.

He was a squire in his prime. Across three counties he was known for his open door, his roaring fires and, most of all, for the quality of his claret. The Mac Carthy cellars, a cool, vaulted labyrinth of stone beneath the house in County Cork, held vintages that could make a Frenchman weep. Now they were haunted.

Not by groaning spectres, not by clanking chains. Casks were found tapped, not smashed but with a neat, drilled hole. The finest bottles left their designated bins and turned up rearranged into puzzling, almost artistic patterns. A '45 Lafite was found perched atop a barrel of porter like a ruby crown. And if you stood still long enough down there, you heard no moaning, only the faint, echoing sound of a satisfied chuckle and the rustle of tiny, quick feet.

One butler swore he had been pinned by his own coattails to a rack of '63 Burgundy. Another claimed a battalion of corks had bombarded him like tiny cannonballs. They were proud men, unflappable professionals; they went down determined and came back white-faced, their dignity in tatters. Higgins, the last of them, mumbled something about a little man before he packed his bags.

Justin poured himself a glass of whisky, from the decanter on the sideboard, not from the cellar. "Enough," he said to the empty room. The honour of Ballinacarthy was at stake, and the integrity of his vintages with it. He would beard this lion, or this leprechaun, in its den.

He took no candle; the cellar had modern gas lamps. He unlocked the heavy iron-bound door, went down the worn stone steps and stood among the ranks of barrels and the silent battalions of bottles, their shoulders dusty. For a long moment he listened. The drip of a distant tap. The house settling. Perhaps it was all fancy, a collective madness.

Then, on the floor before the oldest cask, a pre-Phylloxera claret he called "The Patriarch," he saw a tiny, perfect puddle of ruby wine. Beside it lay a thimble.

Justin was annoyed, and he was also amazed, and he very nearly laughed. He strode forward. "Alright," he called, firm but not unkind. "The game's up. Show yourself. You've terrorised my staff and pilfered my best stock. I'll have a word."

Nothing. Then, from directly behind a barrel of amontillado, a voice like dry leaves and creaking wood: "Terrorised, is it? And pilfered? A harsh tongue for a Mac Carthy, and you a man famed for his hospitality."

Justin spun round.

"Naggeneen," said the figure perched on the spigot of the amontillado barrel, and tipped his hat. "Cluricaune of Ballinacarthy Cellars, at your service. Though it seems my service, keeping your barrels sound and your wine from turning to vinegar, isn't appreciated."

He was no taller than Justin's boot. A coat of faded green, breeches of brown leather, a hat that might once have been pointed and was now crumpled like a forgotten mushroom. Slung over his shoulder like a soldier's rifle, a tiny, finely wrought silver spigot. And the face: that was what Higgins had mumbled about on his way out. "A little man with a face like a wrinkled walnut and an impudent grin." Higgins had been exact. The eyes, sparkling with a millennium of cunning, he had left out.

(In case you are picturing a leprechaun: don't. The leprechauns are cobblers, cousins at most. Cluricaunes are spirits of the wine cellar, born from the very breath of fermentation. Neglect a cellar and one will leave; care for it and it becomes a kingdom. Justin learned all of this that night, in the course of a long argument.)

Justin, to his own surprise, made a slight bow. "Justin Mac Carthy. And I appreciate sound wine well enough. It's the midnight tastings and the assault on my staff I could do without."

"Assault!" Naggeneen squeaked, indignant, and took a comically large leap to the top of The Patriarch; his tiny boots made no sound. "That pompous Higgins! He came down with a mind to board up my favourite nook! A man who thinks a '28 port should be served at room temperature deserves all the corks he gets."

"Your nook?" Justin leaned against a rack.

"Aye, behind the '09 Château Margaux. A fine year, that. A bit forward, but with a heart of oak. You should open a bottle soon." Naggeneen patted the huge cask beneath him. "This old fellow, though. He and I have an understanding. I watch over him, he lets me have the odd sip to ensure he's ageing properly. Symbiosis, your learned guests would call it."

Justin smiled. "And the rearranging?"

"Art!" said the Cluricaune. "Balance! That rack of Mosel was all wrong, too heavy on the left. I corrected it. And that Sauternes, languishing in the dark corner? Shameful. I gave it a place of honour. You should be thanking me."

At some point a bottle of '57 brandy appeared, remarkably good, produced by Naggeneen from a hiding place Justin swore had not been there that morning. Over it the two of them argued like barristers.

"I've been here since the first stone was laid," Naggeneen said, more quietly now. "Saw your great-great-great-grandfather broach his first cask of poteen. A violent brew, that. I improved his stock, gently. A nudge here, a blending there. The Mac Carthy reputation was built, in part, on my palate."

Whether that great-great-great-grandfather ever knew who was blending his poteen, Naggeneen did not say, and Justin did not ask.

"And in return?"

"In return, I have a home. A drop of the finest to keep the damp from my bones. And peace. Until these interlopers with their dusters and their inventories come clattering about, treating the wine like lumber!"

"A truce, then." Justin raised his brandy snifter. "The cellar is your domain. I shall instruct all future staff that the wine is solely my concern. They are not to touch, move, or taste a single drop without my express word. They may only fetch what I specifically request."

Naggeneen eyed him with suspicion. "And you? Will you be clumping down here, disturbing the peace?"

"Only for consultation," Justin said, grinning. "I suspect my education in oenology is sorely lacking. I may need a tutor."

The grin came back to the Cluricaune's face, wider than before. He swung the silver spigot off his shoulder and tapped it against Justin's glass with a delicate *ping*. "A tutor, is it? Well now. For a suitable retainer, say, a thimbleful of The Patriarch on the first frost of each winter, and a promise of no more infernal gas lamps, they dry the air terribly, I might be persuaded."

"The lamps stay," Justin said firmly. "But you shall have a cask of your own. The '71 Shiraz. It's bold enough to suit you."

Naggeneen's eyes gleamed. He spat on his tiny hand and held it out, and Justin, with solemn ceremony, wrapped his own little finger around it.

From that night on there were no more resignations.

At dinner, guests heard Justin say things like, "No, not the '65. The '66 is singing tonight, I can feel it in the stones," and he was proven right, again and again. His wine pairings were uncanny, his sense of when a bottle had reached its peak became legendary, and his hospitality, famous before, became more famous still. Downstairs, Naggeneen pointed out a cask with a faint whisper of taint that no one else could have detected, and saved him a fortune. Justin carried new acquisitions down for appraisal and listened closely to the verdicts, which came with the withering authority of a Parisian wine critic. Sometimes the Cluricaune looked almost content.

O'Sullivan, for his part, sometimes saw from the corner of his eye the master sitting on an upturned crate deep in the vaults, seemingly talking to a barrel. Occasionally he found two glasses set out where only one had been fetched. He was far too well-trained to mention it. What he thought of it, you would have to ask him.

One winter's night they shared the agreed-upon thimble of The Patriarch by the light of a single guttering candle. The gas lamp was off; Justin had turned it off out of politeness.

Naggeneen looked up at the vaults. "You know," he said quietly, "they think we're all gone. The Cluricaunes. That men like you, with your ledgers and your science, have no room for us anymore."

Justin swirled the ancient wine in his glass. "Then they've never tasted a wine cared for by one," he said. "Ballinacarthy has room, Naggeneen. For as long as the vines grow and the barrels age."

The Cluricaune nodded and took a minuscule sip. He was satisfied, deeply so, and for once there was no mischief in his smile. He lifted the thimble. "To the wine," he said.

"To the guardian of the wine," Justin replied, and touched his glass to the thimble.
