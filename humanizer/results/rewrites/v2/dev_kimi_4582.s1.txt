A woman was beaten for a loaf of bread, and Nobody carried her to the constable.

"The Bigwigs are currently debating the jurisdiction of domestic disputes," the constable told him. "No aid can be rendered until the cousin council reaches consensus."

"But she bleeds," Nobody said quietly.

"Then let her bleed until they decide," the constable replied, not unkindly, but with the exhausted resignation of a man who had also learned that he was Nobody.

"It is *my* prerogative to set the dock fees," Reginald Bigwig would shout behind the mahogany doors.

"Your dock fees impoverish the merchants who pay *my* rents," Old Man Bigwig would retort.

"And neither of you considers the cost of the constabulary I must fund to keep order when your policies breed desperation," Millicent would cry, invariably slamming her ledger shut.

Their voices carried through the keyhole into the antechamber like the screeching of jays over a carcass. Nobody met them only when necessity forced his hand. Each autumn, when the rains came, he trudged the muddy road to the Manor House, hat in hand, to petition for the maintenance of the floodwall, and waited while the family quarreled. The Bigwigs lived upstream, where the water ran cleaner. They governed from the Manor House, a family rather than an institution, passing decrees from father to son to cousin as casually as one might pass the salt. Old Man Bigwig measured his power in acres of disputed boundary lines. Young Reginald (you have heard him already) collected tariffs the way boys collect stamps. Cousin Millicent controlled the town's purse strings with the devotion of a miser counting phantom coins.

They would emerge hours later, faces flushed with the triumph of stalemate, to find Nobody standing quietly, his hat rotating in work-worn hands. They would look through him and address the wall above his head.

"The floodwall repairs will be deferred another season."

"The matter requires further study."

"The family is united in its decision to prioritize other concerns."

"Thank you for your patience, Mr...?" Reginald would say, the pause theatrical, the surname deliberately swallowed.

"Nobody, sir," he would answer.

"Quite."

The river spoke in languages Nobody understood—gurgling over stone, whispering against the pilings of his dock, roaring when spring rains swelled its banks. For forty years he had lived with his ear tuned to these voices, pulling silver fish from the current, mending the nets that fed his neighbors, raising three children on the bank where the willows wept into the water. The town of Millbrook knew him simply as Nobody, a name that had stuck to his grandfather and now clung to him like river-mist.

He would walk home from the Manor through streets that grew shabbier with each passing year. The schoolhouse, once bright with the sound of children reciting sums and scriptures, stood empty now, its windows boarded after Cousin Millicent had diverted the teacher's salary to fund a new gate for the Manor. The children of Millbrook ran feral through the alleys instead, learning arithmetic by counting stolen apples, learning history from tavern songs that celebrated the Bigwigs' ancestors while cursing their own empty bellies.

His daughter, Clara, came home one evening with knuckles bloody from fighting a boy who had tried to steal her river-smoothed stone—a treasure, you understand, that she used to practice letters in the mud.

"He said reading is for Bigwigs," she told her father. "He said we're only good for working and dying."

Nobody held her close.

Below his dock the current had grown sluggish, choked with debris the Bigwigs had forbidden the council to dredge. The river was sick, and the fish were fewer. With no school to occupy them and no work to dignify them, the young men of Millbrook turned to the river not for sustenance but for smuggling. They moved contraband past the Bigwigs' sleeping constables, then spent their coins in the gin shops that proliferated where the mill had closed. Fights became common. Theft became casual.

Winter came hard that year, and with it the first rumors of illness in the upstream villages. Nobody heard the gossip at the market: a fever that started with a cough, a wasting sickness that turned strong men into phantoms. He walked to the Manor once more, this time not for walls but for warning.

"The river carries more than fish," he told the servant who admitted him. "It carries what we put into it. The Bigwigs should know—there is sickness above. We must close the upstream intake. We must burn the refuse properly, not dump it where the current brings it back to us."

The servant looked at him with pity. "The family is in session, Mr. Nobody. They are discussing the inheritance of the north woodlot. It is a crisis of some urgency."

He left his warning written on a scrap of paper, weighted with a stone from his pocket. He never knew if they read it. He suspected they used it to steady a wobbling table leg.

Spring arrived with false warmth, and with it the pestilence. It came not as a storm but as a thief, slipping into the hovels that clustered near the stagnant backwaters where the Bigwigs' neglected drains emptied. The fever struck Clara first, then his son Thomas, then the neighbors' children, one by one, until the coughing filled the night like the croaking of doomed frogs.

Nobody worked without rest, ferrying the sick across the river to the old herb-woman who lived on the far bank, boiling water that he suspected was poisoned by the very source of life he had trusted all his days. He watched the Bigwigs' carriage race past on the high road, bound for the country estates where the air was presumed pure, the horses' hooves throwing up dust that settled on the corpses being wrapped in shrouds down below. In the worst week, the death cart came daily.

He walked home past the dying and the dead, past the stagnant pools where mosquitoes rose like curses, and sat on his dock as the sun set, dipping his hands into the current. The water ran dark, carrying the ashes of the burning shacks downstream, away from the Bigwigs' sight. Thomas remained inside, weak but alive, sleeping fitfully. There was work to do tomorrow—nets to mend, perhaps, or a wall to build with his own hands where the Bigwigs' wall had failed. Heavy, this sorrow of his, and his alone to carry.

When Clara died, on a morning, Nobody did not take her to the death cart. He carried her upstream, past the broken floodwall, past the empty schoolhouse with its peeling paint, past the constable's office where the jail stood empty because the prisoners had all sickened and been released to die at home. He walked until his boots touched the manicured gravel of the Manor House drive.

The gates were locked, manned by guards in masks soaked with vinegar. But Nobody had worked the river long enough to know the secret paths. He came to the garden wall where the lilacs grew, and there, in sight of the windows where the Bigwigs still argued, he dug a grave for his daughter.

He dug with his hands, because he had sold his spade to buy medicine that never came. He dug until the soil stained his fingers, and when the hole was deep enough, he laid Clara down, wrapped in his best net, the one he had been saving for the day the fish returned in abundance.

A face appeared at a window—Millicent Bigwig, perhaps, or a servant.

"Move along! This is private property. Move along, or it will be the constable!"

Nobody stood. His clothes were ragged, his face gaunt. He did not rotate his hat in his hands. He let it fall to the grass, looked up at the window, and spoke in a voice that carried over the garden wall, clear and cold as the river in winter.

"I am not Nobody," he said. "I am Elias. I am the man who mends your world while you break it. I am the father who buried wisdom because you stole its schoolhouse. I am the man who understands, now, that your chaos is not accident but appetite. You consume and you quarrel, and we die of your indigestion."

The face withdrew. Elias filled the grave. He placed a stone at its head—not a Bigwig stone, marked with lineage and claim, but a river stone, smooth and gray, marked with nothing but the passage of water. Behind the window the argument resumed, quieter now, perhaps: whether to quarantine the town, or simply wait for the poor to stop inconveniencing them with their dying.
