“Pinned,” one of them swore. “By my own coattails, to a rack of the ’63 Burgundy.”

Another came up the cellar steps with a different story. “Corks. A whole battalion of them, and they came at me like tiny cannonballs.”

Proud men, unflappable professionals, every one of them. They went down with their resolve hardening and came back up with their dignity in tatters.

“*…and whilst I have the utmost respect for your person and your household, sir,*” Justin Mac Carthy read aloud in the library, his voice a rich baritone, “*the persistent and…* *aethereal*… *interference in the wine cellar makes the execution of my duties quite impossible. I regret to inform you that I shall be departing at first light. Yours, etc.*”

He laid it back on the polished oak desk. Higgins. The fifth letter of resignation in as many months. Exasperating, utterly exasperating, and this from a man with nerves of steel, forged, or so he’d claimed, in the service of a Bengal Lancer. Before he packed his bags, Higgins had simply mumbled: “A little man with a face like a wrinkled walnut and an impudent grin.”

A squire in his prime, Justin was known across three counties for his open door, his roaring fires and, most of all, the unparalleled quality of his claret. Beneath the great house of Ballinacarthy, in County Cork, lay the Mac Carthy cellars, legendary, a cool, vaulted labyrinth of stone holding vintages that could make a Frenchman weep. Yet now they were haunted. Casks were found tapped, not vandalistically, but with a neat, drilled hole. The finest bottles were moved from their designated bins and rearranged into puzzling, almost artistic patterns. A ’45 Lafite, the ’45, mind you, sat perched atop a barrel of porter like a ruby crown. No groaning spectres, no clanking chains, no ghostly moan. Only the faint, echoing sound of a satisfied chuckle and the rustle of tiny, quick feet.

Justin poured himself a glass of whisky, not from the cellar, but from the decanter on the sideboard. “Enough,” he said to the empty room. The honour of Ballinacarthy was at stake, and the integrity of his vintages with it. He would beard this lion, or this leprechaun, in its den.

The new butler, a quiet man named O’Sullivan, received one single instruction, and a strange one: “The cellar is Mr. Mac Carthy’s private sanctuary. You enter only on his command, and you touch nothing. If you hear… laughter… pay it no mind.”

Justin took no candle. The cellar had gas lamps, modern ones, you understand, and their hiss was a familiar whisper in the stillness. He unlocked the heavy iron-bound door and went down the worn stone steps. The lamps cast a soft, yellow glow over the ranks of barrels and the silent battalions of bottles, their shoulders dusty with time.

For a long moment he stood and listened. The drip of a distant tap. The sigh of the house settling. Perhaps it was all fancy, then. A collective madness.

Then he saw it. Before the oldest cask, the pre-Phylloxera claret he called The Patriarch, a tiny, perfect puddle of ruby wine. Beside it, a thimble.

Part annoyed, part in sheer wonder, Justin laughed and strode forward. “Alright,” he called, firm but not unkind. “The game’s up. Show yourself. You’ve terrorised my staff and pilfered my best stock. I’ll have a word.”

Silence. Then, from directly behind a barrel of amontillado: “Terrorised, is it? And pilfered? A harsh tongue for a Mac Carthy, and you a man famed for his hospitality.”

Justin spun.

“Naggeneen,” said the voice, a voice like dry leaves and creaking wood, and its owner, perched on the spigot of the amontillado barrel, tipped his hat. “Cluricaune of Ballinacarthy Cellars, at your service. Though it seems my service—keeping your barrels sound and your wine from turning to vinegar—isn’t appreciated.”

He stood no taller than Justin’s boot. A coat of faded green, breeches of brown leather, a hat that might once have been pointed but was now crumpled like a forgotten mushroom. The face was indeed like a wrinkled walnut, lit by two eyes that sparkled with a millennium of cunning. Slung over his shoulder like a soldier’s rifle was a tiny, finely wrought silver spigot.

Justin, to his own surprise, made a slight bow. “Justin Mac Carthy. And I appreciate sound wine well enough. It’s the midnight tastings and the assault on my staff I could do without.”

“Assault!” Naggeneen squeaked, and took a comically large leap to the top of The Patriarch. His tiny boots made no sound. “That pompous Higgins! He came down with a mind to board up my favourite nook! A man who thinks a ’28 port should be served at room temperature deserves all the corks he gets.”

“Your nook?” Justin leaned against a rack.

“Aye, behind the ’09 Château Margaux. A fine year, that. A bit forward, but with a heart of oak. You should open a bottle soon.” Naggeneen patted the huge cask beneath him. “This old fellow, though. He and I have an understanding. I watch over him, he lets me have the odd sip to ensure he’s ageing properly. Symbiosis, your learned guests would call it.”

Justin smiled. “And the rearranging?”

“Art!” declared the Cluricaune. “Balance! That rack of Mosel was all wrong, too heavy on the left. I corrected it. And that Sauternes, languishing in the dark corner? Shameful. I gave it a place of honour. You should be thanking me.”

They debated like two barristers, the squire and the spirit, over a bottle of remarkably good ’57 brandy that Naggeneen produced from a hiding place Justin swore hadn’t been there that morning.

“We’re not leprechauns, mind,” Naggeneen said. “Cousins. Cobblers, the lot of them.”

“And a Cluricaune?”

“A spirit of the wine cellar. Born from the very breath of fermentation.”

“Of the cellar,” Justin said.

“Neglect a cellar and you’ll drive one of us away. Care for it, and it’s a kingdom.”

“And in return?” Justin asked.

“In return, I have a home. A drop of the finest to keep the damp from my bones. And peace. Until these interlopers with their dusters and their inventories come clattering about, treating the wine like lumber!”

“A truce, then,” Justin proposed, raising his brandy snifter. “The cellar is your domain. I shall instruct all future staff that the wine is solely my concern. They are not to touch, move, or taste a single drop without my express word. They may only fetch what I specifically request.”

Naggeneen eyed him suspiciously. “And you? Will you be clumping down here, disturbing the peace?”

“Only for consultation,” Justin said, grinning. “I suspect my education in oenology is sorely lacking. I may need a tutor.”

The Cluricaune’s arch grin came back, wider than ever. He swung the tiny spigot off his shoulder and tapped it against Justin’s glass with a delicate *ping*. “A tutor, is it? Well now. For a suitable retainer—say, a thimbleful of The Patriarch on the first frost of each winter, and a promise of no more infernal gas lamps, they dry the air terribly—I might be persuaded.”

“The lamps stay,” Justin said firmly. “But you shall have a cask of your own. The ’71 Shiraz. It’s bold enough to suit you.”

Naggeneen spat on his tiny hand and held it out. Justin, with solemn ceremony, wrapped his own little finger around it, and the pact was sealed.

From that night, the resignations ceased.

Justin’s hospitality became more famed still. His wine pairings were uncanny, his intuition for when a bottle was at its peak legendary. “No, not the ’65,” he would say, and his guests would marvel. “The ’66 is singing tonight, I can feel it in the stones.” And he’d be proven right.

If O’Sullivan sometimes saw, from the corner of his eye, the master sitting on an upturned crate deep in the vaults, seemingly talking to a barrel, or if he occasionally found two glasses set out where only one had been fetched, he was far too well-trained to mention it.

The peculiar friendship flourished. Some nights, near to content, Naggeneen would point out a cask with a faint whisper of taint only his keen senses could detect, and save a fortune. Justin, in turn, brought down each new acquisition for the appraisal of his tiniest guest and listened intently to the judgments, delivered with the withering authority of a Parisian wine critic.

One winter’s night they shared the agreed-upon thimble of The Patriarch by a single, guttering candle, the gas lamp politely off. Naggeneen looked up at the vaults.

“I’ve been here since the first stone was laid,” he said. “Saw your great-great-great-grandfather broach his first cask of poteen. A violent brew, that. I improved his stock, gently. A nudge here, a blending there. The Mac Carthy reputation was built, in part, on my palate. You know, they think we’re all gone. The Cluricaunes. That men like you, with your ledgers and your science, have no room for us anymore.”

Justin swirled the ancient wine. “Then they’ve never tasted a wine cared for by one,” he said. “Ballinacarthy has room, Naggeneen. For as long as the vines grow and the barrels age.”

The Cluricaune nodded, took a minuscule sip and smiled. He lifted his thimble. “To the wine.”

“To the guardian of the wine,” Justin replied.

The thimble and the glass met, one small, one large.
