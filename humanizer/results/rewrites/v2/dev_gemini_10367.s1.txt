### I. The Ledgers

"Mr. Frederick," said the man on the porch of the Hawkins manor. His voice did not seem to come from his throat, but rather vibrated from the air around him. "You have come for the ledgers. But the ledgers are written in a language of loss that you may find difficult to balance."

This was Mr. Polleto—a man, if he can be called such—ostensibly the "caretaker" hired by the distant cousins of the Hawkins line. He was tall, dressed in a suit of deep charcoal. His skin had the quality of wet parchment, and his eyes... they were too large for his face, shimmering with a gelatinous, unblinking intensity.

Revolted by him, by his mere proximity, I clutched my briefcase.

"The town is unwell, Mr. Polleto," I managed to say.

"The town is transitioning," he replied with a thin, lipless smile. "Change is often mistaken for malady by those who cling to the old shapes."

The grass at his feet was not merely dead; it was translucent, the blades turning into a clear, brittle glass that shattered under the weight of his shadow.

He led me inside, and the air grew worse.

"Your room is on the second floor, Mr. Frederick," he said, and led the way. As we walked the corridors we passed the center of the house, where a large section of the hallway had been crudely, violently bricked over. The mortar was fresh, yet the bricks themselves looked ancient, as if they were aging centuries in a matter of days.

### II. One Missing Brick

That night, the "Steepleford Cough" began to plague me. It was the same sound I had heard coming from the cottages in the village: a dry, rattling sound, as if the villagers were breathing in ground glass.

I could not sleep. The house groaned, but not with the settling of timber; it sounded like a moan. Behind the bricked-up wall, something was thudding, rhythmically. I took my candle and ventured into the hallway.

As I approached the masonry, I saw a gap: a single missing brick near the eye-level of a tall woman. I peered through. (You would have done the same.) I expected darkness. I expected a hidden room. What I found was a pair of eyes.

They were the eyes of Amber Maria Hawkins. I knew her from the portrait in the foyer, a beautiful, spirited woman who had supposedly "fled to the continent" years prior. But the woman behind the wall had not fled. She had been entombed.

Her eyes were no longer human. They were the color of clouded amber, glowing with a fierce, radioactive spite. She did not blink. She did not scream. She simply *looked*. Where her sight landed on the opposite wall, the wallpaper blistered and turned to grey ash.

The town elders had bricked her in alive, perhaps even Polleto himself, for some perceived transgression, and her rage had become a literal force of erosion. No disease of the soil, then, the blight on Salter's Lane: her vengeance, projected. She was looking through the house, through the hills, staring at the trees until they forgot how to be wood. She was staring at the people of Steepleford until their lungs forgot how to hold air.

### III. The Boar's Head

"I'll go no further than the Boar's Head, sir," the carriage driver had said, his eyes darting toward the rising slope of Salter's Lane. "The air up there... it doesn't sit right in the lungs."

"Superstition," I told him, with all the arrogance of youth.

I was a young solicitor then, arrived in Steepleford in the autumn of 1882 and tasked with the unenviable job of settling the accounts of the Hawkins estate. The village was tucked into a fold of the hills. Upon my arrival, the first thing I noticed was the silence. It was not the peaceful quiet of the countryside, but a heavy, pressurized stillness. The driver would not take me past the village green.

I walked the remaining mile toward the Hawkins manor, and it was then that I first beheld the trees of Salter's Lane. They were ancient oaks and elms that should have been in the vibrant throes of autumnal gold. Instead, they were a uniform, ghostly grey. But they were not dead in the ordinary sense. As I passed them, a light breeze stirred, and a branch, thick as a man's thigh, did not break or sway. It simply *dissolved*. It crumbled into a fine, microscopic powder that hung in the air like a veil of ash.

I reached out to touch the trunk of a great oak, and my finger passed through the bark as if it were made of compressed smoke. There was no wood beneath, only a desiccated, calcified dust.

By the time I reached the manor, my black coat was turned grey by the fallout of the forest.

### IV. Stone and Iron

Every morning, I sweep a fine, grey dust from my pillow.

"A skin condition," my doctors call it. "A remnant of some valley fever you contracted in your youth." I know better. I am not whole.

I never went back. I resigned from the firm and moved to the heart of London, seeking the density of stone and the permanence of iron. I am an old man now, Frederick of a lineage that once valued logic and the hard certainties of the law. The fire in my study has burned down to a heap of glowing embers, and for forty years I have tried to bury all of it beneath the soot of London.

"Steepleford is no longer on the maps," they tell me. "The postal carriages bypass the valley entirely." The heavy mists, they say, have reclaimed the geography of that cursed place.

Amber Maria is still staring; I know that. Somewhere, in a place that no longer exists on any map, she sits behind a wall of weeping brick, her amber eyes fixed on the horizon, unmaking the world. And Mr. Polleto is walking. He is walking toward the cities, toward the crowds, carrying with him the silence of Salter's Lane.

Sometimes, when the room is very quiet, I hear the sound of a brick being tapped from the inside. You will remember the thudding. The walls of my own study are feeling remarkably thin.

### V. The Village Green

I ran down the stairs, which crumbled like sandbags under my boots. I burst out of the front door and into Salter's Lane.

The moon was out. The trees were no longer recognizable as trees. They were pillars of ash, held together by the lack of wind. As I ran, the displacement of air caused by my body caused the oaks to collapse in my wake. A hundred-year-old grove vanished into a cloud of grey soot as I passed, a silent, thundering avalanche of dust.

In the village, the lights were dim. I saw a face at a window: a woman whose features were blurring, her nose and chin softening into a featureless mask of grey skin.

I reached the village green and found my carriage driver. The horses were terrified. He was dead, slumped over his seat. But he was not a corpse in the way I understood it. He was a hollow shell, his clothes draped over a pile of fine white powder.

I grabbed the reins and whipped the horses. Their hooves struck the ground with a muffled, hollow sound. We drove through the mist, through the falling ash of the world, until the air finally began to bite with the cold sting of a winter frost.

### VI. The Corridor

I turned from the wall to flee, but Mr. Polleto was standing at the end of the corridor. He held no candle, yet he was visible by a faint, sickly luminescence that clung to his skin.

"She is quite dedicated, is she not?" Polleto whispered.

"You did this," I gasped, falling against the wall. Where my hand touched the stone, the masonry felt soft, like rotting fruit. "You let them bury her."

"I did not suggest it," Polleto said, stepping closer. With every step he took, the floorboards beneath him shriveled. "I merely facilitated the equilibrium. I am a catalyst, Frederick. I accelerate what is already there. The town held a secret rot; I simply gave it the agency to manifest. Amber Maria provides the will; I provide the medium."

He reached out a hand. His fingers were elongated, the tips fading into a grey mist. Not a man, then, nor even a ghost. A manifestation of entropy; the end of things, in person. I was terrified of him.

"The trees were the first to go," he continued, his voice now a chorus of dry whispers. "They are simple structures. Humans are more complex. They take longer to turn to dust. But look at your hands, Frederick. Look at the edges of your life."

I looked down. My fingernails were translucent. The skin of my wrists was beginning to flake away into that same fine, grey powder.

I do not know how I found the strength to move. I lunged past him. He did not move to stop me; he merely watched with those unblinking, gelatinous eyes.
