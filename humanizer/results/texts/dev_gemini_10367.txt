The fire in my study has burned down to a heap of glowing embers, much like the memories I have tried for forty years to bury beneath the soot of London. They tell me that Steepleford is no longer on the maps—that the postal carriages bypass the valley entirely, and the heavy mists have reclaimed the geography of that cursed place. I am an old man now, Frederick of a lineage that once valued logic and the hard certainties of the law, yet I find my hands trembling as I take up the pen.

I must record the truth of Salter’s Lane. I must speak of Amber Maria Hawkins and the man—if he can be called such—named Mr. Polleto. For if I do not, the sickness that started in that soil may one day find its way into the wider world, and humanity is ill-equipped for a blight that consumes not the flesh, but the very essence of form.

### I. The Invitation to the Hollow

I arrived in Steepleford in the autumn of 1882. I was a young solicitor then, tasked with the unenviable job of settling the accounts of the Hawkins estate. The village was tucked into a fold of the hills in a manner that felt less like shelter and more like a wound.

Upon my arrival, the first thing I noticed was the silence. It was not the peaceful quiet of the countryside, but a heavy, pressurized stillness. The carriage driver refused to take me past the village green.

"I'll go no further than the 'Boar’s Head,' sir," he had said, his eyes darting toward the rising slope of Salter’s Lane. "The air up there... it doesn't sit right in the lungs."

I dismissed his superstitions with the arrogance of youth. I walked the remaining mile toward the Hawkins manor, and it was then that I first beheld the trees of Salter’s Lane.

They were ancient oaks and elms that should have been in the vibrant throes of autumnal gold. Instead, they were a uniform, ghostly grey. But they were not dead in the traditional sense. As I passed them, a light breeze stirred, and I witnessed a horror of biology: a branch, thick as a man’s thigh, did not break or sway. It simply *dissolved*. It crumbled into a fine, microscopic powder that hung in the air like a veil of ash.

I reached out to touch the trunk of a great oak, and my finger passed through the bark as if it were made of compressed smoke. There was no wood beneath—only a desiccated, calcified dust that tasted of copper and old pennies.

By the time I reached the manor, my black coat was turned grey by the fallout of the forest.

### II. The Presence of Polleto

The manor was a jagged silhouette against the bruised sky. It was there I met Mr. Polleto.

He was standing on the porch, ostensibly the "caretaker" hired by the distant cousins of the Hawkins line. To look at him was to experience a profound sense of vertigo. He was tall, dressed in a suit of such deep charcoal that it seemed to absorb the dim light. His skin had the quality of wet parchment, and his eyes... they were too large for his face, shimmering with a gelatinous, unblinking intensity.

"Mr. Frederick," he said. His voice did not seem to come from his throat, but rather vibrated from the air around him. "You have come for the ledgers. But the ledgers are written in a language of loss that you may find difficult to balance."

As he spoke, I felt a wave of sudden, violent nausea. It was as if my very cells were recoiling from his proximity. The grass at his feet was not merely dead; it was translucent, the blades turning into a clear, brittle glass that shattered under the weight of his shadow.

"The town is unwell, Mr. Polleto," I managed to say, clutching my briefcase.

"The town is transitioning," he replied with a thin, lipless smile. "Change is often mistaken for malady by those who cling to the old shapes."

He led me inside, and the air grew worse. It was thick with a smell I can only describe as the scent of a sun-bleached bone. He showed me to a room on the second floor, but as we walked the corridors, I noticed something peculiar about the architecture. In the center of the house, a large section of the hallway had been crudely, violently bricked over. The mortar was fresh, yet the bricks themselves looked ancient, as if they were aging centuries in a matter of days.

### III. The Gaze from the Wall

That night, the "Steepleford Cough" began to plague me. It was the same sound I had heard coming from the cottages in the village—a dry, rattling sound, as if the villagers were breathing in ground glass.

I could not sleep. The house groaned, but not with the settling of timber. It sounded like a moan. I took my candle and ventured into the hallway, drawn by a rhythmic thudding coming from behind the bricked-up wall.

As I approached the masonry, I saw a gap—a single missing brick near the eye-level of a tall woman.

I peered through.

I expected darkness. I expected a hidden room. What I found was a pair of eyes.

They were the eyes of Amber Maria Hawkins. I knew her from the portrait in the foyer—a beautiful, spirited woman who had supposedly "fled to the continent" years prior. But the woman behind the wall had not fled. She had been entombed.

Her eyes were no longer human. They were the color of clouded amber, glowing with a fierce, radioactive spite. She did not blink. She did not scream. She simply *looked*.

The intensity of her gaze was physical. Where her sight landed on the opposite wall, the wallpaper blistered and turned to grey ash. I realized then that the blight on Salter’s Lane was not a disease of the soil. It was the projection of her vengeance. She had been bricked alive by the town elders—perhaps even by Polleto himself—for some perceived transgression, and her rage had become a literal force of erosion.

She was looking through the house, through the hills, staring at the trees until they forgot how to be wood. She was staring at the people of Steepleford until their lungs forgot how to hold air.

### IV. The Entropy of Mr. Polleto

I turned to flee, but Mr. Polleto was standing at the end of the corridor. He held no candle, yet he was visible by a faint, sickly luminescence that clung to his skin.

"She is quite dedicated, is she not?" Polleto whispered. 

"You did this," I gasped, falling against the wall. Where my hand touched the stone, the masonry felt soft, like rotting fruit. "You let them bury her."

"I did not suggest it," Polleto said, stepping closer. With every step he took, the floorboards beneath him shriveled. "I merely facilitated the equilibrium. I am a catalyst, Frederick. I accelerate what is already there. The town held a secret rot; I simply gave it the agency to manifest. Amber Maria provides the will; I provide the medium."

He reached out a hand. I saw that his fingers were elongated, the tips fading into a grey mist. I realized with a jolt of pure, primal terror that Polleto was not a man, nor even a ghost. He was a manifestation of entropy. He was the personification of the end of things.

"The trees were the first to go," he continued, his voice now a chorus of dry whispers. "They are simple structures. Humans are more complex. They take longer to turn to dust. But look at your hands, Frederick. Look at the edges of your life."

I looked down. My fingernails were translucent. The skin of my wrists was beginning to flake away into that same fine, grey powder.

### V. The Flight from Steepleford

I do not know how I found the strength to move. Perhaps it was the sheer, instinctual rejection of the nothingness Polleto represented. I lunged past him. He did not move to stop me; he merely watched with those unblinking, gelatinous eyes, as if my escape were merely a temporary delay in an inevitable process.

I ran down the stairs, which crumbled like sandbags under my boots. I burst out of the front door and into the nightmare of Salter’s Lane.

The moon was out, and in its pale light, the forest was a graveyard of ghosts. The trees were no longer recognizable as trees. They were pillars of ash, held together by the lack of wind. As I ran, the displacement of air caused by my body caused the oaks to collapse in my wake. A hundred-year-old grove vanished into a cloud of grey soot as I passed, a silent, thundering avalanche of dust.

In the village, the lights were dim. I saw a face at a window—a woman whose features were blurring, her nose and chin softening into a featureless mask of grey skin. The illness of Steepleford was the slow dissolution of the self.

I reached the village green and found my carriage driver. He was dead, slumped over his seat. But he was not a corpse in the way I understood it. He was a hollow shell, his clothes draped over a pile of fine white powder.

I grabbed the reins and whipped the horses. They were terrified, their hooves striking the ground with a muffled, hollow sound. We drove through the mist, through the falling ash of the world, until the air finally began to bite with the cold, honest sting of a winter frost.

### VI. The Lingering Ash

I never went back. I resigned from the firm and moved to the heart of London, seeking the density of stone and the permanence of iron. 

But I am not whole. 

Every morning, I sweep a fine, grey dust from my pillow. My doctors call it a skin condition, a remnant of some "valley fever" I contracted in my youth. But I know better. I know that Amber Maria is still staring. I know that somewhere, in a place that no longer exists on any map, she sits behind a wall of weeping brick, her amber eyes fixed on the horizon, unmaking the world.

And I know that Mr. Polleto is walking. He is walking toward the cities, toward the crowds, carrying with him the silence of Salter’s Lane. He is the guest who never leaves, the shadow that dissolves the floor, the man who watches while the trees turn to dust.

Sometimes, when the room is very quiet, I hear the sound of a brick being tapped from the inside. And I realize that the walls of my own study are feeling remarkably thin. 

The blight is not gone. It is merely patient. And in the end, we are all just dust waiting for the wind.
